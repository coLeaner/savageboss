Ya, welcome-oh. Every July 26, no matter how much code I owe mansastock or how many tickets sitting for MAS Pharmacy, I close the laptop small and step outside — because the whole of Paynesville smells like fried plantain and sounds like a marching band by 9 in the morning. That's how you know: Liberia don turn one more year.

This year make it 179.

## Where July 26 Comes From

Liberia declared its independence on July 26, 1847, becoming the first republic in Africa to do so. The nation was founded by freed Black settlers from the United States and the Caribbean, alongside the indigenous peoples who were already home here, and Joseph Jenkins Roberts became its first president. The flag they raised — eleven red and white stripes with a single white star on a field of blue — earned Liberia its nickname, **the Lone Star**. The motto still sits under the seal today: *"The love of liberty brought us here."*

That history is heavy, and Liberians don't pretend otherwise. But July 26 was never just a history lesson. It's a day the whole country agrees to stop and remember it's still standing — through war, through Ebola, through hard economy — and that counts for something.

## What the Day Looks Like

The official program is always the same shape, even as the details shift. Flag-raising at dawn. A march-past at the stadium — these days it's usually the SKD Sports Complex, though in recent years the government has taken turns hosting the big national program in a different county, so the celebration doesn't only belong to Monrovia. Soldiers, police, students, and civil society groups parade in the sun while the President delivers the independence oration, the one speech every Liberian half-expects to hear quoted back to them at work the next day.

And then there's the white.

Come July 26, you'll see it everywhere — white shirts, white lappa, white everything. Nobody legislates it; it's just something Liberians do, a quiet way of dressing the whole nation in one color for one day, peace showing on the outside for once.

## The Food (the Real Program, If You Ask Me)

Forget the stadium — the real independence program happens in the kitchen. By midday every yard smells the same:

- **Jollof rice**, cooked in the biggest pot the house owns
- **Cassava leaf** and **palm butter**, simmering since morning
- **Dumboy** with soup thick enough to stand a spoon in
- **Potato greens** and fried plantain on the side
- Cold drinks — and if uncle in a good mood, something stronger for the elders

Neighbors cook extra on purpose, because plates travel between houses all afternoon. Nobody in Paynesville buys their own lunch on the 26th; you eat whichever kitchen calls your name first.

## Paynesville, My Corner of It

Where I stay, independence doesn't wait for the TV broadcast from the stadium. The block has its own program — small speakers out, kids in their new clothes chasing each other round the yard, korkor sellers doing their best business of the year, a football match that starts friendly and ends serious. Church holds a thanksgiving service in the morning before anybody touches food, because whatever else changes, we still say thank God first.

It's not flashy. But it's honest — and it's the version of July 26 I actually grew up inside.

## Still Building

I spend most days building small, quiet tools — mansastock, client sites, keeping MAS Pharmacy's data straight — the kind of work that doesn't make headlines but keeps things running. There's something in that, that fits the day. Liberia wasn't built loud either. It got built one stubborn year after another, by people who kept showing up.

179 years later, I'm still here, still building. Happy Independence Day, Liberia — long may the Lone Star fly. 🇱🇷
